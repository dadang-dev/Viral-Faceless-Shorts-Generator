## Day 1 — 3 spending habits quietly making you poorer

**Voice-over:**
> You're not bad with money. You just have three habits working against you without you noticing.
> Number one: subscription creep. That's fourteen dollars for a streaming app, nine for a fitness app, five for cloud storage you forgot you even signed up for — until you're paying for five apps you forgot existed.
> Number two: convenience spending. Every 'I'll just order it, I'm tired' feels like ten dollars in the moment. But ten dollars, four nights a week, is about a hundred and seventy dollars a month.
> Number three: rounding it off in your head. You think of an eighteen-dollar coffee run as 'like twenty bucks.' Do that three times a week, and you've quietly spent over two hundred dollars a month on 'basically nothing.'
> None of these make you careless. They just run quietly in the background. This week, try naming just one of them out loud. That's it. Naming it is the first habit you break.

## Day 2 — Why you feel broke even when you're not

**Voice-over:**
> Here's something that messes with almost everyone: you can get a raise, and still feel broke six months later. That's called lifestyle creep.
> It's not one big decision. It's a slightly nicer apartment — two hundred dollars more a month because 'I can afford it now.' It's upgrading from a used car to a new lease. It's ordering the appetizer AND dessert instead of just picking one, because why not.
> Each choice feels small and reasonable in the moment. But stack them up, and your spending rises exactly as fast as your income — sometimes faster.
> The fix isn't depriving yourself. It's just noticing: did my spending go up because I actually wanted this, or because I could?
> That one question, asked honestly, catches more leaks than any budgeting app.

## Day 3 — The 'girl math' trap — what it's really costing you

**Voice-over:**
> You've probably heard 'girl math' — if I paid in cash it's free, if it's under twenty dollars it doesn't count. It's funny. It's also quietly expensive.
> The joke works because it's true for almost everyone, not just one group. Our brains are wired to round small numbers down to basically nothing. A seven-dollar iced coffee doesn't feel like spending. A twelve-dollar phone case 'doesn't count' because it was an accident purchase.
> The problem isn't the joke. It's when the joke becomes the actual system you use to decide what to buy.
> Try this instead: for one week, write down every 'it doesn't really count' purchase in one place. Not to guilt yourself — just to see the real number.
> Most people are shocked not by one purchase, but by what all the 'free' ones add up to by Sunday.

## Day 4 — The real reason you buy things you don't need

**Voice-over:**
> Emotional spending isn't a willpower problem. It's a wiring problem.
> When you're stressed, bored, or exhausted, buying something gives your brain a real, measurable hit of relief — for about ten minutes. Maybe it's a late-night online order, maybe it's a 'treat yourself' coffee run after a hard meeting.
> That's not weakness. That's biology. The issue is that the relief is short, and the bill isn't.
> Here's a habit that actually works: before you check out, ask 'what am I feeling right now?' — not 'do I need this.' Half the time, naming the feeling — tired, bored, anxious — is enough to make the urge pass on its own, right there in the checkout screen.
> You don't need more discipline. You need one honest question before the checkout button.

## Day 5 — Subscription creep — the habit nobody checks

**Voice-over:**
> Quick challenge: without looking, guess how many subscriptions you're currently paying for. Now go check your bank statement.
> Most people guess three or four — maybe streaming and one app. Most people actually have eight to twelve, once you count cloud storage, a forgotten trial that converted to paid, a fitness app you used twice in January.
> Subscriptions are designed to be forgettable — that's not an accident, that's the business model. The moment you stop noticing the charge, it becomes permanent income for someone else.
> Here's the habit: once a month, open your statement and just look at every recurring charge for ten seconds each. Not to cancel everything — just to see it.
> You don't have to be extreme about this. You just have to actually look.

## Day 6 — Why saving money feels impossible

**Voice-over:**
> If saving money feels impossible, it's probably not a math problem. It's a scarcity mindset problem.
> When money has felt tight before — even years ago, even a stressful semester in college — your brain can get stuck treating every dollar like it might be the last one for a while. That makes saving feel unsafe, not smart.
> That's why 'just spend less' advice doesn't work for a lot of people. It's not addressing the actual fear underneath the spending — it's not that they don't know saving is good, it's that saving doesn't FEEL safe.
> A small habit that helps: start with an amount so small it feels almost pointless — even five dollars a week. The goal isn't the amount. It's teaching your brain that money going into savings doesn't mean losing it.
> Once that feels safe, the amount can grow. But safety has to come first.

## Day 8 — Pay in four is still a bill

**Voice-over:**
> That “pay in four” button can make a purchase feel smaller without making it cheaper. You see today’s payment. Your calendar sees the next three. And if you split another purchase before those clear, the due dates can pile up right when rent or your phone bill hits. This isn’t about never using buy now, pay later. It’s about seeing the whole obligation before you agree to it. Open your payment schedule and look at what’s already due before your next paycheck. Then ask: if I had to see every payment at once, would I still press buy? A small first payment is still the first payment—not the full price.

## Day 9 — Your balance isn’t your spending money

**Voice-over:**
> That balance in your banking app? It might not all be yours to spend. Not because the bank made a mistake, but because tomorrow’s bills aren’t always obvious on today’s screen. Rent, a card payment, your phone plan—autopay can be useful, but several withdrawals can land close together. Here’s the habit: put your next payday and every automatic payment on the same calendar. Look at what will be left after those bills, not just the number you see right now. If a payment date is going to be a problem, check your options with the provider before it arrives. Money you can see is not always money that’s unclaimed.

## Day 10 — Rent is not a personality test

**Voice-over:**
> People love to tell renters to stop buying coffee. But if housing is your biggest bill, cutting one small treat won’t make the housing market cheaper. That doesn’t mean you have no choices. It means you need to compare the real cost of each one. A listing can show lower rent and still cost more once you add utilities, parking, and the trip to work. Before you decide a place is affordable, put those costs beside what you actually take home. If the numbers are tight, that’s information—not a verdict on your character. The cheapest listing isn’t always the cheapest month. What housing cost surprised you after you moved in?

## Day 11 — Minimum due is not paid off

**Voice-over:**
> There’s a reason your credit card statement shows more than one number. “Minimum due” tells you what you need to pay to stay current. It does not mean the balance is gone. If you carry that balance, interest may keep adding up under your card’s terms. So don’t just scan for the smallest number and close the app. Find three things: the minimum due, the full statement balance, and the interest charged. If you can pay the statement balance in full, check how your card’s grace period works. If you can’t, make a plan around the real balance—not just the minimum. The minimum is a deadline, not a finish line.

## Day 12 — An ad isn’t a background check

**Voice-over:**
> A slick ad in your feed can look like proof that a store is real. It isn’t. A look-alike website can copy the photos, logo, and countdown timer of a brand you trust. The deal feels urgent because it’s supposedly about to disappear. That’s the moment to leave the ad and check the seller somewhere else. Search the company’s name with “scam” or “complaint.” Check the return policy and the payment options. If the seller insists on gift cards, crypto, or a wire transfer, walk away. Taking an extra minute isn’t overthinking. It’s the part of shopping that the ad hopes you’ll skip.

## Day 13 — The bill you forgot to expect

**Voice-over:**
> A car repair can be unexpected. The fact that a car will cost money again isn’t. The same goes for an insurance renewal, a phone replacement, or another expense that doesn’t show up every month. Those bills can feel like emergencies when your monthly plan has no place for them. Try one small change: pick one irregular expense you know is coming, or likely to come. Put its date—or a reminder to check it—on your calendar. Give it its own line in your plan. You don’t have to predict the exact amount. You’re just making room for the category before it catches you off guard. Which bill always seems to arrive “out of nowhere”?

## Day 14 — A job shouldn’t make you pay to get paid

**Voice-over:**
> A message says you can earn money by liking videos or “boosting” products. The app shows your earnings climbing. It might even pay you a little at first. Then comes the catch: to unlock the rest, you have to deposit your own money. That’s not a side hustle. That’s how a task scam works. A number inside an app is not a paycheck you can spend. If a supposed job wants you to pay to get paid, stop. Don’t send another deposit to recover the first one. Check the company through a source you found yourself, and report the offer to the FTC. Real work shouldn’t make you buy access to your wages.
